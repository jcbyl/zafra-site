The Story

za·fra noun In Spanish, la zafra — the harvest, the season a crop comes in. From the Arabic safra, “a journey” — the one the crews make to the fields at harvest, and the one these products make to your home.

Puerto Rico once grew coffee for the courts of Europe. Today the island grows about 36,000 quintales a year and drinks 200,000. By the growers’ own estimates, half the crop is never picked — not for want of land or trees. For want of crews.

The farmers who stayed have the heirloom trees and the knowledge. What they lack is a way to the market — while nearly six million Puerto Ricans on the mainland pay a premium for the coffee they grew up with.

Zafra is that way. The harvest of the mountain, taken whole. Zafra is the harvest; Areito is the gathering — one word for what the mountain produces, one for the people who produce it. Commerce under Zafra, community under Areito, one coffee between them.

Three things were missing, so we built three things. Crews, so the crop comes off the trees. Mills, so cherry becomes lots worth paying for. A channel to the world, so the money comes home.

The ask of the market is simple: a bag of coffee that tastes like home and pays the farmer like a partner. Coffee leads. Honey, cacao and vanilla follow — same brand, same box, same shelf.

Rise, Fall, Rebuild

1736
Coffee reaches Puerto Rico from the French Antilles and climbs into the Cordillera.
The golden age
By the end of the 19th century, Puerto Rican coffee is poured in the courts of Europe and at the Pope’s table, shipped out from Ponce.
1899
Hurricane San Ciriaco.
The long decline
Sugar, war and industrialization pull the economy to the coast. The coffee highlands go quiet.
2017
Hurricane María flattens a large share of the island’s coffee trees. The farmers who stayed spend the years since replanting.
Today
The heirloom genetics, the mountain craft and the pickers’ knowledge are still here. Coffee here is identity.
The rebuild
Crews that pick, mills that cup, a price printed on every lot, and the money coming home. That is this company.

The Cordillera

Running west to east through the island’s heart, the Cordillera Central rises past 4,000 feet. It is the rainiest corner of Puerto Rico outside El Yunque, home to most of the island’s endemic species and most of its protected forests. Coffee, honey, cacao and vanilla all grow here, in mountain towns like Adjuntas, Jayuya, Utuado, Lares and Yauco.

The timeline tells what happened to this range. The lots tell what comes next.

Who We Sell To

Zafra roasts on the island and ships direct — subscriptions first, online retail beside them. On the island: the Zafra Mercantil café, hotel shops, and wholesale to cafés, hotels and restaurants. Off the island: roasted wholesale, and green export for lots with a committed buyer. No commodity lane. Nothing in bulk, ever.

Why Puerto Rico

The island grows less than a fifth of the coffee it drinks. That is the point: every pound traceable to a named Puerto Rican farm is structurally unlike anything else on the shelf. Shade-grown heirloom Arabica, hand-picked ripe at altitude, milled and roasted on the island — coffee with every right to stand with Kona and Jamaica Blue Mountain. And today almost none of it can be bought off the island. Scarcity is the supply side. The brand is how it is honored.
